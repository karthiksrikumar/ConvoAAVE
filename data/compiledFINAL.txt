Let me talk about the habitual be first. 'Cause that's the most famous one.

When I say "He be workin," that don't mean right now.

That mean he got a job. He stay employed. That's his routine.

But if I say "He workin," that mean right now. At this moment.

See the difference? That's aspect. That's time spread out.

Standard English don't have that. Y'all gotta say "He usually works."

We just say "He be workin." One word. Efficient. Precise.

That habitual be is African. Came over on the ships.

Survived. Thrived. Became ours.

Some people say it's lazy. It ain't. It's complex.

You gotta know the context. The timing. The unspoken rules.

If I say "She be late," that mean she always late. Habitual.

If I say "She late," that mean right now she ain't on time.

You feel me? That's a whole grammatical distinction.

Standard English can't do that without extra words.

So who really the lazy one? I'm just sayin.

Now let me talk about the remote past been. Stressed B-E-E-N.

This one different. This one go way back.

If I say "I been ate," that mean I ate a long time ago.

Not just now. Not yesterday. Way back.

But here's the trick. You gotta stress the been. B E E N.

If you say it regular, it just mean "I have eaten."

But if you stretch it out? "I B E E N ate." That's different.

That mean I ate hours ago. Maybe yesterday.

Some linguists call it the remote past. I call it genius.

We needed a way to say "that happened way back."

So we made one. That's language creation. That's innovation.

And white linguists in the 1960s "discovered" it like we ain't been usin it forever.

Funny how that work. We been speakin. They been catchin up.

This one express something finished. Completed. Done did.

"I done told you a hundred times." That mean I told you. Repeatedly. And I'm tired.

The "done" add emphasis. Add frustration. Add "I'm done with this conversation."

Standard English would say "I have already told you."

Where the emotion in that? Where the attitude?

"Done" carry weight. Attitude. History.

"I done fixed the car." That mean it's fixed. And I'm proud. Or tired.

Context matters. But the "done" tell you it's complete.

No question. No maybe. Done is done.

Let me talk about the double negative. Or what they call negative concord.

They say "don't use no double negatives." That's prescriptivist nonsense.

In AAVE, double negatives reinforce each other. They don't cancel out.

"Ain't nobody got time for that." Mean exactly what it sound like.

Nobody. Zero. No one got time.

Standard English "nobody has time" mean the same thing.

But we say it with more feeling. More rhythm.

"Ain't no way." That mean there is no way. Period.

They say two negatives make a positive. Not in our language.

Two negatives make a stronger negative. That's logic too.

Just different logic. Different rules. Both valid.

Let me talk about copula deletion. That's fancy for droppin "is" and "are."

"He nice." Not "he is nice." You know what I mean.

"She funny." Not "she is funny." The meaning clear.

We drop the copula in certain places. Not all places.

You can say "You crazy," but you can't say "That's what you."

Rules exist. They just different.

We drop the present tense copula. Not the past tense.

"She tall" work. "She was tall" work. You can't say "She tall yesterday."

That don't work. So there's a rule. See?

Linguists been studyin this. Labov. Rickford. Smitherman.

Black scholars been sayin this for decades.

But the world still call it "broken English." Ignorant.

Let me show you how it really work.

First thing. The habitual be.  
That's our most famous move. And folks always get it wrong.  
When we say "He be workin," that ain't the same as "he workin."  
"He workin" mean right now. At this moment. Present tense immediate.  
"He be workin" mean he got a job. He stay employed. Habitual action.  
That be carryin the meaning of "usually" or "regularly."  
We don't need extra words. The be do the work.  
"He be tired" mean he stay tired. Like every day after work.  
Not just right now. You hear the difference? That's precision.  
Linguists call it the habitual aspect. We just call it talkin.  
Same thing with "She be callin me." That mean she call on the regular.  
Not one time. Repeatedly. Consistently.  
You can't get that meaning from standard English without addin words.  
We built it into the verb. Efficient.  

Now lemme hit you with the remote past. Done.  
When we say "I done told you," that mean I told you already.  
And not just yesterday. Back then. Way back.  
But also with a sense of completion. It's over. It's finished.  
"I done ate" mean I finished eatin. Don't offer me no plate.  
That done marks the action as fully in the past. No doubt.  
Compare to "I ate." That just state a fact.  
But "I done ate" got attitude. It got finality.  
Like "why you askin? I done handled it."  
That's the energy. That's the flair.  

Then you got the stressed been. This one tricky.  
You gotta put the emphasis on the word. BEEEEEN.  
"She BEEEN ready" don't mean she ready now.  
It mean she been ready for a long time. And still ready.  
That BEEEN carry the whole weight of duration.  
"She BEEEN knew that" mean she knew that before you said it.  
Way before. Like hours or days or years before.  
You can't do that in regular English without a whole sentence.  
We do it with one word and a shift in tone.  
That's economy. That's genius.  
Linguists call it the remote present perfect or somethin.  
We just call it tellin you how it is.  

Let me talk about the copula deletion. Fancy term for leavin out "is" and "are."  
"She pretty" instead of "she is pretty."  
"They crazy" instead of "they are crazy."  
We drop the verb to be in present tense. Don't need it.  
The meaning still clear. Context do the job.  
But here's the thing. We don't drop it all willy nilly.  
There's rules. We don't drop it at the end of a sentence.  
"That's what it is." You can't say "that's what it."  
Nah. That don't work. And we know it don't work.  
So the rule is: you can delete it in the middle, not the end.  
That's grammar. That's systematic.  
It ain't sloppy. It's different. There's a difference.  

Now the negative inversion. This one smooth.  
"Ain't nobody got time for that." You notice the order?  
Usually it's "nobody ain't got time." But we flip it.  
The negative come first. "Ain't nobody."  
That's for emphasis. That's for drama.  
Same thing with "Don't nobody care." Instead of "nobody cares."  
That double negative ain't a mistake. It's intensifying.  
In standard English, two negatives make a positive.  
In AAVE, two negatives make a stronger negative.  
"Ain't never" don't mean "ever." It mean "never ever."  
"Ain't no" don't mean "some." It mean "absolutely none."  
Different logic. Different system. Still valid.

"He finna act up." That mean trouble comin. Get your camera.  
"You finna make me mad." That mean you on the edge.  
One more move and it's on. That's precision timing.

"I'm tryna eat" mean I want to eat. I'm attemptin.
